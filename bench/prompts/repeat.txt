Thermodynamics describes how energy moves through physical systems and how work is extracted from differences in temperature, pressure, and chemical potential. When two reservoirs at different temperatures are connected by a heat engine, thermal energy flows spontaneously from the hotter body toward the colder one, while a fraction of that energy can be converted into mechanical work. No cyclic process can convert heat from a single reservoir completely into work without leaving a compensating change elsewhere, because the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases. At the microscopic scale, temperature reflects the average kinetic energy of particles in random motion, and entropy measures the number of accessible microstates consistent with the macroscopic state. Equilibrium is reached when every intensive variable—temperature, pressure, and chemical potential—becomes uniform across all phases in contact, so no further net macroscopic flux of heat, volume, or matter can occur between parts of the system.
